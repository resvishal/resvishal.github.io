PhD Applications Abroad: I Was Doing Everything Except the Right Things

Created on April 07, 2026

  • Final Thought

When I started applying for PhD positions abroad during my master’s, I thought I was being serious about it.

I was not.

I had decided I wanted to go abroad — not just for research, but also for the kind of academic environment where people are more open to ideas and discussions. That part was clear to me.

What wasn’t clear was how the process actually works.

I didn’t know how to identify labs that genuinely matched my interests. I didn’t know how to write a proper first email. I didn’t know what a strong CV looked like in this context. And I definitely didn’t understand research papers well enough to talk about them with confidence.

So I did what felt like effort.

I wrote long emails to PIs.

Mistake: Writing long emails thinking it shows sincerity.

Very long emails.

I thought explaining everything in detail would show sincerity. In reality, it just made my emails harder to read. I was also hesitant to attach my CV in the first email — which, looking back, makes no sense. That is literally the one document that helps someone evaluate you quickly.

I was also filtering poorly.

Instead of being clear about which labs actually made sense for me, I would get interested in a lab, read a bit, and convince myself that this was “the one.” That kind of emotional attachment doesn’t help — it just biases your judgment.

The outcome was predictable.

No shortlists. Barely any replies. Mostly silence.

At that point, it’s easy to think:

“Maybe they don’t consider international applicants seriously.”

I thought that too.

But that wasn’t the real issue.

What Actually Changed

Two things shifted my approach.

First, I stopped focusing only on getting selected and started paying attention to the work itself.

There are people doing genuinely interesting research, and many of them are open to bringing in students who are willing to learn. That realization changed how I looked at labs — from places I need to get into to places where I could contribute.

Second, I got hands-on exposure through a project at CSIR.

Working on something real — writing drafts, analysing results — made it very obvious where I stood. It also made one thing clear: I had been thinking about applications without building enough clarity around my own work.

The Core Mistake{#the-core-mistake}

I delayed action because I was unsure.

I kept thinking I needed:

  • a better profile
  • more understanding
  • more confidence

before I could reach out properly.

Key Insight: Delay due to uncertainty is more damaging than rejection.

In reality, that delay just slowed everything down.

What I Would Do Differently{#what-i-would-do-differently}

If I had to restart, I would simplify the process:

1. Fix the CV first

This is non-negotiable.

Your CV is the first filter. It should be clean, structured, and easy to scan. You don’t need to artificially inflate it with random certifications. Just present what you have properly.

Most people underestimate how much clarity in a CV matters.

2. Keep the first email short

You are not writing an SOP.

A good first email should:

  • clearly state your research interest
  • briefly connect it to their work
  • attach your CV

Mistake: Trying to impress instead of being clear.

That’s it.

If they’re interested, they’ll reply. If not, no amount of extra paragraphs would have changed that.

3. Don’t over-filter before acting

You don’t need the “perfect lab” before reaching out.

You need a reasonable match and a clear reason to contact them.

Overthinking this stage just delays useful feedback.

Final Thought{#final-thought}

The biggest shift for me was simple:

Stop trying to feel ready. Start moving.

I spent close to a year doubting whether I was good enough to reach out properly. That hesitation cost me more than any rejection would have.

If you’re starting out, don’t repeat that.

Build your CV.
Write the short email.
Reach out.

You’ll learn more from a few ignored emails than from months of overthinking.
