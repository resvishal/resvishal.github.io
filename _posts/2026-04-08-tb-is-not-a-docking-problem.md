Tuberculosis remains one of the leading infectious killers despite decades of drug discovery efforts. The standard computational workflow—target selection, docking, and molecular dynamics—has produced thousands of compounds with strong predicted binding. Yet clinical success has been limited. The issue is not the absence of targets or ligands, but the gap between controlled molecular predictions and the biological reality in which *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* survives.

In vivo, the pathogen resides in granulomas, where drug penetration is uneven and treatment requires long durations. This creates conditions for poor efficacy and resistance, independent of binding strength. Any meaningful progress must therefore address drug distribution, treatment timelines, and resistance evolution, rather than optimizing affinity alone.

In my view, most work in this space is still focused on the wrong layer of the problem. Binding is necessary, but not decisive. The real challenge is connecting molecular design to clinical constraints—penetration, persistence, and resistance. Until that shift happens, improvements will remain incremental.
